Chapter 1

Introduction to Statistics

Statistics is a branch of applied mathematics which deals with the collection, organization, presentation, analysis, and interpretation of data.

Two Branches of Statistics Memorize

Descriptive Statistics
Deals with collection and presentation of data; uses summarizing values like measures of central tendency and variation to describe group characteristics.
Inferential Statistics
Deals with predictions and inferences based on analysis and interpretation. Common tools: t-test, z-test, ANOVA, chi-square, Pearson r.

Types of Variables Memorize

Categorical / Qualitative

Classified according to some attributes or categories.

  • Gender, eye color, religion
  • Blood type, civil status
  • Political preferences, year level
  • Course, profession, socio-economic status
Categories may be ordered e.g., Poor → Fair → Good → Excellent
Numerical / Quantitative

Classified according to numerical characteristics. Often grouped into class intervals.

  • Height, age, pulse rate
  • No. of children, speed
Discrete — obtained by counting (no. of children, no. of patients)
Continuous — obtained by measuring (temperature, height, weight)

4 Scales of Measurement Memorize

  • 1
    Nominal
    Classifies into categories; numbers show elements are different but NOT ordered or ranked. Example: religion, gender.
  • 2
    Ordinal
    Ranks individuals by degree; shows order but intervals are NOT equal. Example: anxiety level (0=not anxious → 3=high), performance rating.
  • 3
    Interval
    Equal distances between scores; NO absolute zero. Example: temperature (0°C ≠ no temperature), aptitude scores.
  • 4
    Ratio
    Same as interval scale but HAS an absolute zero. Examples: height, weight, area, speed, amount of money.

Key Terms Memorize

Variable
A numerical characteristic or attribute associated with the population being studied.
Population
The entire group of people, animals, places, things, or ideas to which conclusions will be applied.
Sample
A subgroup of the population.
Parameter
A numerical measure that describes a characteristic of a population. Example: "The population mean IQ of students is 105."
Statistic
A numerical measure that describes a characteristic of a sample. Example: "The sample mean IQ of 45 students is 105."
Key Analogy: Parameter : Population = Statistic : Sample
Chapter 2

Collection of Data

There is no single best method for collecting data. The choice depends on: research design, type of data, time available, and financial capacity.

7 Common Methods of Collecting Data Memorize

  • 1
    Interview Method
    Direct — personal interview; good for small groups (<30). Indirect — telephone interview; expensive if many respondents, may be biased (people without phones excluded).
  • 2
    Questionnaire Method
    A list of well-planned questions administered personally or mailed. 8 types (see below).
  • 3
    Empirical Observation
    Data through seeing, hearing, tasting, touching, smelling. Used in psychology and anthropology. Participant vs. Non-participant; Controlled observation = in a lab.
  • 4
    Test Method
    Uses standard valid, reliable, usable tests. Examples: aptitude tests, IQ tests, achievement tests. Widely used in psychological and psychiatric research.
  • 5
    Experiment Method
    Observation done in a laboratory = controlled observation.
  • 6
    Registration Method
    Data from government agencies: NSO, LTO, DepEd, CHED, SEC, Supreme Court, etc.
  • 7
    Use of Mechanical Devices
    Social/educational: camera, projector, video tape, tape recorder. Medical: X-ray, microscope, ultrasound, CT scan. Astronomy: telescope, barometer, radar.

8 Types of Questionnaire Memorize

  • 1
    Guided-Response
    The respondent is guided in making their reply. Example: "Have you helped the needy? Yes/No. If Yes, proceed to Q2."
  • 2
    Recall Type
    Asks for recalled information. Example: Age, sex, civil status, length of stay in a community.
  • 3
    Recognition Type
    Respondent picks from given options. Example: "Which figure is a square?"
  • 4
    Dichotomous Type
    Only two choices. Example: "Do you live alone? Yes / No"
  • 5
    Multiple Choice Type
    Several answer choices with one correct answer. Example: "Which color is for apple? a.Red b.Orange c.Blue..."
  • 6
    Multiple Response Type
    Respondent can tick/check more than one answer. Example: "What appliances do you have at home?"
  • 7
    Free Response Type
    No guidance; respondent answers in their own style.
  • 8
    Rating Scale Type
    Respondent rates on a scale. Example: Attitude toward Mathematics (1-Disagree to 4-Strongly Agree).

Sampling Methods Memorize

Cluster Sampling
Area is divided into clusters; groups (not individuals) are randomly selected. Best when area is too large and population too big. Example: selecting 3 of 13 regions at random for a nationwide study.
Purposive Sampling
Respondents chosen based on their specific knowledge of the required information. Example: selecting senior citizens for a town's historical study.
Quota Sampling
Commonly used in opinion polls. Researcher gathers from a convenient location until a required number is reached. Example: salesman interviewing females in a mall until 2,000 responses are gathered.
Convenience Sampling
Fastest method; may be biased. Example: using telephone interviews (excludes those without phones) or a teacher using their own students.
Chapter 3

Organization & Presentation of Data

Note: A Frequency Distribution Table (FDT) is only needed when the number of observations is more than 30. Data in FDT = Grouped Data. Data NOT in FDT = Ungrouped Data.

Types of Data for Tabulation Memorize

  • 1Frequency Distribution
  • 2Correlated Data
  • 3Time Series Data

Constructing a Frequency Distribution Table (FDT) Understand

  • 1
    Find the Range (R)
    R = Highest Score – Lowest Score
  • 2
    Compute number of intervals (n)
    n = 1 + 3.3 log N
  • 3
    Compute class size (i)
    i = R ÷ n  (round up to whole number)
  • 4
    Build the intervals
    Start with lowest score. Add (i−1) to get upper limit. Add i to lower limit for next interval, repeat.
  • 5
    Count frequencies
    Use tally marks for each class interval.

Key FDT Terms Memorize

Class Mark (Midpoint)
= (Lower Limit + Upper Limit) ÷ 2    Example: For 6–14 → (6+14)÷2 = 10
Class Boundary (Exact Limit)
Lower boundary = Lower Limit − 0.5    Upper boundary = Upper Limit + 0.5    Example: 6–14 → Boundary: 5.5–14.5

3 Data Types

Frequency Distribution
Organizes data into class intervals with frequencies. Used when N > 30.
Correlated Data
Table showing two or more frequencies for easy comparison. Example: number of cases by both gender and religion.
Time Series Data
Shows change of a variable over time. Best presented with a line graph.

6 Methods of Graphing Memorize

  • 1
    Bar Graph
    Used to compare data and determine which class appears most. Uses rectangular bars.
  • 2
    Line Graph
    Used for trends: increase in sales, rise/fall of temperature, enrolment per year, population comparison.
  • 3
    Pie Chart
    Compares different parts of a whole. Circular chart divided into proportional sectors.
  • 4
    Frequency Histogram
    Bar graph for frequency distributions (no gaps between bars).
  • 5
    Frequency Polygon
    Line graph connecting midpoints of frequency histogram bars.
  • 6
    Cumulative Frequency Ogive
    Line graph of cumulative frequencies. Used to determine median and percentiles.
Quick Rule: Trends over time → Line Graph · Parts of a whole → Pie Chart · Comparison across categories → Bar Graph
Chapter 4

Measures of Central Tendency

The three measures of central tendency are: Mean, Median, and Mode.

A. Ungrouped Data

📐 Mean (Arithmetic Average)
Formula
x̄ = (Σ xᵢ) ÷ N

Where: x̄ = mean, xᵢ = each observation, N = total number of observations

Example: Grades: 78, 88, 89, 90, 95 → x̄ = (78+88+89+90+95) ÷ 5 = 440 ÷ 5 = 88
📏 Median (Middle Value)

The midpoint of an ordered array. Symbol: Md

  • Step 1Arrange data in ascending or descending order.
  • Odd NMedian = the single middle value.
  • Even NMedian = mean of the two middle values.
Odd Example: 10 12 14 16 23 24 25 33 34 35 41 45 50 → Md = 24 (7th value)
Even Example: 2 5 5 6 8 9 9 10 → Md = (6+8)÷2 = 7
🎯 Mode (Most Frequent Value)

The observation that appears the most number of times.

No mode: No value appears more than once.
Unimodal: One mode. Example: scores → Mode = 20
Bimodal: Two modes. Example: 3 and 5 both appear 5 times → Mode = 3 and 5
Modal class = interval with highest frequency. Modal value = how many times it appears.

B. Grouped Data (Frequency Distribution Table)

📐 Mean (Grouped)
Formula
x̄ = Σ(fᵢ · xᵢ) ÷ N

Where: xᵢ = class mark, fᵢ = frequency of class, N = total observations

Steps: 1) Find class marks   2) Multiply each class mark by its frequency (fᵢ × xᵢ)   3) Add all products   4) Divide by N
📏 Median (Grouped)
Formula
Md = LMd + [ (N/2 − <CFb) ÷ fMd ] × i
SymbolMeaning
LMdLower boundary of the median class
N/2Half of total observations
<CFbCumulative frequency BELOW the median class
fMdFrequency of the median class
iClass size
Finding the Median Class: The median class is the class interval that contains the N/2-th value. Look at the cumulative frequency column to find which class it falls in.
🎯 Mode (Grouped)
Formula
Mo = LMo + [ Δ1 ÷ (Δ1 + Δ2) ] × i
SymbolMeaning
LMoLower class boundary of the modal class
Δ1Difference: frequency of modal class − frequency of class BEFORE it
Δ2Difference: frequency of modal class − frequency of class AFTER it
iClass size
Modal classThe class interval with the HIGHEST frequency

Part V — Slovin's Formula & Stratified Sampling

What is Slovin's Formula for finding sample size?
n = N ÷ (1 + Ne²) · where n = sample size, N = population, e = margin of error as decimal
800 students passed the scholarship exam. Total who took the exam is 5,423. Find the margin of error. Give answer to the whole number, tenths, and hundredths place.
e² = [(5423÷800)−1] ÷ 5423 = 5.779÷5423 = 0.001065 → e = √0.001065 = 0.03264 · Whole: 3 · Tenths: 2 · Hundredths: 6
450 students qualified to enroll in IT, CS, and IS. Margin of error is 2.5%. How many students enrolled? (n = ?)
n = 450 ÷ (1 + 450×0.000625) = 450 ÷ 1.28125 ≈ 351 (check with your professor — exam answer was 626)
How many samples are needed if the target population is 1,750? A margin of error of 5% is allowed.
n = 1750 ÷ (1 + 1750×0.0025) = 1750 ÷ 5.375 = 325.58 ≈ 325
A student researcher uses 5% margin of error. Sample size obtained is 250. What is the population?
Rearranged: N = n(1 + ne²) = 250(1 + 250×0.0025) = 250(1.625) = 406.25 ≈ 406 (exam answer was 325 — confirm with professor)
In a school: Freshman=400, 2nd year=350, 3rd year=500, 4th year=600. Sample size needed = 400. What is the required margin of error?
N = 1850, n = 400 · e² = [(1850÷400)−1] ÷ 1850 = 3.625÷1850 = 0.001959 · e = √0.001959 = 0.04426 ≈ 4.40%

Using the 3 Purok (N=1,662, total n=670), what is the percentage and sample size of Purok 1 (pop. 812)?

📐 Solved Examples

Slovin's Formula — The Main Formula

Formula
n = N ÷ (1 + Ne²)

n = sample size    N = population    e = margin of error as decimal
Common e values:   5% = 0.05 → e² = 0.0025     2.5% = 0.025 → e² = 0.000625     3% = 0.03 → e² = 0.0009
📌 Find n — Population 1,750, e = 5% (Items 84–85)
n = 1750 ÷ (1 + 1750 × 0.0025)
n = 1750 ÷ (1 + 4.375)
n = 1750 ÷ 5.375
n = 325.58 ≈ 325
📌 Find Margin of Error — n = 800, N = 5,423 (Items 79–81)
Rearrange Slovin's to solve for e
n(1 + Ne²) = N
1 + Ne² = N ÷ n
Ne² = (N ÷ n) − 1
e² = [(N ÷ n) − 1] ÷ N

e² = [(5423 ÷ 800) − 1] ÷ 5423
e² = [6.779 − 1] ÷ 5423
e² = 5.779 ÷ 5423 = 0.001065
e = √0.001065 = 0.03264
e ≈ 0.03264 → whole: 3, tenths: 2, hundredths: 6
📌 Find Margin of Error — School with 1,850 students, n = 400 (Items 88–89)
Given: Freshman=400, 2nd yr=350, 3rd yr=500, 4th yr=600
N = 400+350+500+600 = 1,850    n = 400

e² = [(1850 ÷ 400) − 1] ÷ 1850
e² = [4.625 − 1] ÷ 1850
e² = 3.625 ÷ 1850 = 0.001959
e = √0.001959 = 0.04426
e ≈ 4.40%

Stratified Sampling — 4-Step Process

Step 1 — Total N = sum of all stratum populations
Step 2 — Total n = Slovin's formula using N and e
Step 3 — % per stratum = (stratum pop ÷ N) × 100
Step 4 — Sample per stratum = % (as decimal) × total n
